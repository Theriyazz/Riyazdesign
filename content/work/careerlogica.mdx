## Context

I designed a psychometric-first career guidance platform for the UAE and South Asian markets — a full marketing website and four role-based dashboards, bilingual in English and Arabic.

CareerLogica is CareerNaksha rebuilt for the UAE, on one observation: students across the region pick their career stream under pressure from family, peers and assumption, with almost no data about themselves in the decision. That inheritance is why this case study can show its work. I wasn't guessing at what students needed — I was looking at a product already in use, and at everything it got wrong. Where a screen here differs from its predecessor, the difference is the argument.

## 01 — The problem worth solving

When I got this brief, one question kept me up: why does this need to exist at all?

Every year, thousands of UAE students choose their career stream under pressure from parents, peers and assumptions — not from data, not from science, not from any real understanding of who they are. Three groups were being failed at once, in three different ways.

<CardGrid>
  <Card eyebrow="For students" title="No credible tool to decide with">
    No personalised way to connect interests to actual careers — before making a decision they'll live with for years.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="For counsellors" title="Five tools doing one job">
    Managing an entire practice across WhatsApp, email, Google Drive, a calendar and a spreadsheet.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="For schools" title="Zero visibility">
    No tracking, no reporting, no oversight of what happens between a counsellor and their students. All of it manual.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

The challenge wasn't designing three separate tools. It was designing one coherent system that served four completely different mental models at the same time.

### Scope and approach

An existing platform to learn from, and a market it was never built for. I was brought on as the sole freelance UX/UI designer: research, information architecture, wireframing, UI design, the design system, and handoff. No junior support. Every decision in this document traces back to me.

## 02 — What I learned before I designed anything

I'd never designed for the UAE. I didn't know how the education system worked, how families made these decisions, or what a Grade 11 student in Dubai actually worries about. So before anything else, I went and found out.

### Two findings that changed the design

<Findings>
  <Finding n="01" title="The paywall was in the wrong place">
    Almost every competitor let users sign up, complete onboarding, then hit a payment wall at the final step before the dashboard. Users invested effort, then got blocked. That's not a monetisation strategy — it's a trust problem disguised as one.
  </Finding>
  <Finding n="02" title="Onboarding was exhausting people before they started">
    Long forms, dozens of small fields crammed onto a single page. Users were mentally spent before completing the first section. High cognitive load at exactly the moment you're asking someone to be honest about their future.
  </Finding>
</Findings>

### Persona, structure, then layout

**Persona** — one student, drawn from the market research rather than invented: a Grade 11 teenager whose parents are in the room for the decision.

**Sitemap** — four roles mapped against a single information architecture, so one system could serve all of them without becoming four products.

**Wireframes** — the marketing site first, then the dashboards. Structure argued in grey, so the visual work later was about tone rather than about where things go.

## 03 — The decision that shaped the product

Every competitor gated the product behind a paywall. I argued for the opposite: let people in, let them see what the platform actually knows about them, and ask for money only after that value is undeniable.

This wasn't a guess. I'd implemented a freemium model at CareerNaksha and watched it work — users who experienced the assessment first converted better and complained less than users asked to pay upfront.

<Decision
  decision="Free until the value is proven, not gated behind a paywall"
  why="Payment at the last step destroys trust. When someone completes a 15-minute assessment and then hits a paywall, the emotional read isn't 'this is valuable' — it's 'I've been tricked.' Moving the ask to after the first meaningful result means the user is deciding whether to buy more of something good, not whether to gamble on something unknown. Demonstrated value converts better than promised value: a locked dashboard is a promise, a partial result is proof."
  tradeoff="Revenue arrives later in the funnel, and the free tier has to be generous enough to be genuinely convincing — a harder design problem than a paywall at step one. It also had to carry the institutional products: schools won't buy a platform their students haven't used, so free student access is the acquisition channel for the counsellor and school tiers."
/>

### Three separate signup flows, not one with branches

A student, a counsellor and a school are not the same person filling in different fields. One form with conditional branches would have been cheaper to build and worse for all three — a fifteen-year-old wading past credential questions, a counsellor asked what grade they are in.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Student">

  </Card>
  <Card title="Counsellor">
    Experience, expertise, credentials. A professional register — this person is applying, not signing up.
  </Card>
  <Card title="School">
    Institutional details and the structure of who they oversee.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### 4-step onboarding, named after what it builds

The competitor research showed me what not to do: everything on one page, dozens of fields, no sense of progress. I split onboarding into four steps, each with a name that tells the student what they're doing and why — not "Step 2 of 4", but a stage in understanding themselves.

<Steps>
  <Step n="01" name="Identity">Personal details — who you are.</Step>
  <Step n="02" name="Scholar">Educational details — where you are academically.</Step>
  <Step n="03" name="Explorer">Career preferences — what you're drawn to.</Step>
  <Step n="04" name="Achiever">Final goal and purpose — what you're actually trying to reach.</Step>
</Steps>

<Principle name="Chunking (Miller's law)">
  One long form asks the brain to hold too much at once. Four named steps break the same task into pieces the brain can process one at a time — each with its own small sense of completion.
</Principle>

Naming the steps did two things. It reduced the perceived length of the form, because four named stages read as shorter than one long page. And it reframed the task — the student isn't filling in a form, they're building a profile of themselves. The bar opens at 2/4 rather than 0/4 for a related reason: a blank bar reads as a big ask.

## 05 — One system, four products

A student, a counsellor, a school administrator and a platform admin want opposite things from the same product. The student wants clarity and encouragement. The counsellor wants throughput. The school wants oversight. The admin wants control.

One component library. One set of tokens. Density and tone do the differentiating — the student dashboard spacious and encouraging, the counsellor dashboard dense and built for volume. Same parts, different rhythm.

### The student dashboard — the hardest screen in the product

I genuinely did not know where to begin with this one. Too many decisions, too many kinds of user, too many things competing for the same screen. It took two or three full rounds before it settled, and each round taught me something the previous one had missed.

What it settled into is a screen where every element has one job. The greeting and step tag create momentum. The journey tracker answers the first-time anxiety of *what am I supposed to do here.* The counsellor widget seeds the next paid action. Pricing sits in the open, because a product asking a teenager's parents for money should never look like it is hiding the number.

  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Was — three icon styles in one sidebar" title="One icon set, one meaning per icon">

  </Card>

<Principle name="Endowed progress effect">
  People are more likely to finish something when they can see they have already made headway on it. "Your Journey" shows 1 of 3 done rather than an empty tracker — the step the student has already taken is counted and named, so what is left reads as the rest of something started rather than the whole of something not.
</Principle>

Nine screens run on one component library. Every one of them is the same set of parts at a different density.

### Same system, three more brains

The other three roles run on the same library. What changes between them is density and tone, not the parts.

<CardGrid cols="3">
  <Card eyebrow="Counsellor" title="Same system, different brain">
    Counsellors think in workflows, not journeys — throughput, not hand-holding. The home screen leads with status and action: pending students, test codes, verification progress. Student Connect replaces the WhatsApp-email-spreadsheet stack from section 01. Brand Content lets a counsellor put their own logo on a report, which makes them a partner rather than a tenant.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="School" title="Institutional scale, simplified">
    Schools manage hundreds of students and need overview, not depth. Test-code inventory assigned versus unassigned at a glance, a roster filtered by grade, counsellor oversight — and deliberately no individual student detail. Giving an administrator everything creates noise; giving them the right summary creates clarity.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Admin" title="The control room">
    Seven management modules, one principle: density without confusion. Consistent column logic across every table, colour-coded status readable at a glance, and two explicit actions per row — approve or reject, verify or cancel — so no row ever ends in a question.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Decision
  decision="Same components at different densities, not four design languages"
  why="Four roles with four mental models could have justified four tailored interfaces, and on a team with more designers it might have. One library meant a fix landed everywhere at once, and it kept the product legible across roles — a counsellor who opens a student's view to walk them through a result is not learning a second interface mid-conversation. Density and tone carried the differentiation instead: the same card, spaced generously for a fifteen-year-old and packed tight for someone processing forty students a week."
  tradeoff="No role gets a layout perfectly tuned to it. The student dashboard carries more structure than a first-time teenager strictly needs, and the admin tables are less compact than someone living in them all day would build for themselves. A system that fits four roles well fits none of them perfectly, and I'd make the same call again at this team size."

## 06 — Building it in both directions

I'd never designed a bilingual product. I assumed Arabic support meant swapping text and mirroring layout. That lasted about a day.

Every element had to be reconsidered from the perspective of someone who reads right to left — nav bar, section order, button placement, icon direction, where the eye lands first, how a photo reads when its subject now faces the wrong way.

It forced me to stop designing from habit. English had a thousand prior instincts to lean on; Arabic had none, so every call had to be reasoned from scratch — which made the English version sharper too.

Bilingual isn't a feature bolted on at the end. It's a constraint that belongs in the design system from component one, or you build everything twice.

## 07 — What shipped

The product isn't live, so there are no conversion numbers and no retention data. Here's what is real.

<SpecList>
  <Spec label="Scope">140+ screens across five platform roles. Solo — a scope that typically needs a team of two to three.</Spec>
  <Spec label="System">A complete bilingual EN/AR design system, with RTL/LTR built in from day one rather than retrofitted.</Spec>
  <Spec label="Approval">The client approved the final direction after the first major presentation, with no structural revisions. For a 0-to-1 product this complex, that's a real signal.</Spec>
  <Spec label="Timeline">Three months, solo. Not faster work — fewer wrong turns. Owning the problem end to end meant fewer reversals later.</Spec>
</SpecList>

### Working with a non-technical founder

The client knew exactly what he wanted the business to do, and very little about how products get built. That turned out to help — I had to argue every real decision in business terms, not design terms. Why the paywall moved. Why onboarding split into four. Why RTL couldn't wait.

Once the reasoning landed, he stopped reviewing pixels and started trusting direction. That's when the project moved fast.

### What I'd do differently

<Takeaways>
  <Takeaway title="Test with real students">
    Every call was validated with the founder, not the people who'd use it. He knew the market and was usually right, but agreement isn't validation. Next time: five student sessions before locking onboarding.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway title="Build RTL into the system on day one">
    I designed English-first and adapted Arabic after. That cost rework on components that should have been direction-agnostic from the start.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway title="Push back harder on scope in month one">
    The extra month wasn't slow work — it was scope growing without renegotiation. I should have flagged it in week three instead of absorbing it.
  </Takeaway>
</Takeaways>
