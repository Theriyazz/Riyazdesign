## 01 — The problem worth solving

When I got this brief, the first question I asked was how to make it feel premium and trustworthy enough that people would pay. Three groups were being failed at once:

- **Students choose a stream under pressure, not from data**
- **Counsellors run scheduling on WhatsApp and email**
- **Schools have no way to run mass counselling or keep a record for each student**

<Highlight lead="The challenge wasn't three separate tools." strong="It was one coherent system serving four different mental models." />

### Three findings that changed the design

<Findings>
  <Finding n="01" title="The paywall was in the wrong place">
    Competitors let users sign up and finish onboarding, then blocked them with a payment wall at the last step.

    Led to: free until the value is proven.
  </Finding>
  <Finding n="02" title="Onboarding was exhausting people before they started">
    Long forms and dozens of fields left users mentally spent before the first section was done.

    Led to: 4-step onboarding.
  </Finding>
  <Finding n="03" title="The UAE psychometric market was effectively undefined">
    No platform combined a credible assessment with a product anyone would enjoy using.

    Led to: a premium visual system.
  </Finding>
</Findings>

## 03 — The decision that shaped the product

Every competitor said pay first, find out after. I argued the opposite: at CareerNaksha, users who took the assessment before paying converted better and complained less.

<Decision
  decision="Free until the value is proven, not gated behind a paywall"
  why="A paywall after a 15-minute test feels like a trick. After a real result, paying feels like unlocking something they already own."
  tradeoff="Revenue arrives later, and the free tier has to be generous enough to convince."
/>

<Decision
  spaced
  decision="Three separate signup flows, not one with branches"
  why="A student, a counsellor and a school are not the same person filling in different fields."
  tradeoff="Three flows to build and maintain instead of one."
/>

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Student">
    Academic details and interests. Conversational and low-pressure.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Counsellor">
    Experience and credentials. This person is applying, not signing up.
  </Card>
  <Card title="School">
    Institutional details and who they oversee.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<ImageBlock src="/work/careerlogica/signup-flows.avif" alt="Three signup flows branching from a single entry screen — student, counsellor and school" />

<Decision
  spaced
  decision="4-step onboarding, named after what it builds"
  why="Four named steps give the brain one piece at a time (chunking, Miller's law), named after what the student is becoming, not what they're filling in."
  tradeoff="More screens before the dashboard."
/>

<Steps>
  <Step n="01" name="Identity">Personal details — who you are.</Step>
  <Step n="02" name="Scholar">Educational details — where you are academically.</Step>
  <Step n="03" name="Explorer">Career preferences — what you're drawn to.</Step>
  <Step n="04" name="Achiever">Final goal and purpose — what you're actually trying to reach.</Step>
</Steps>

### What I learned

<Takeaways>
  <Takeaway title="Test with real students">
    Every call was validated with the founder, not users. Next time: five student sessions before locking onboarding.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway title="Push back on scope in month one">
    Scope grew without renegotiation. I should have flagged it in week three.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway title="Domain expertise compounds">
    CareerNaksha had already taught me how these platforms behave, so I made better calls faster.
  </Takeaway>
  <Takeaway title="Strategy before pixels">
    Making the freemium call before opening Figma made every later decision easier to defend.
  </Takeaway>
</Takeaways>
